CANADA — NOVA SCOTIA / ESTABLISHED TRAINING EXAMPLE

From simulator to the woods: how two colleges train equipment operators

Nova Scotia Community College and New Brunswick Community College combine theory, simulator practice and field experience in their Mechanized Forest Equipment Operator program. NSCC’s account describes WestFor Management’s role in shaping training around the need for operators of harvesters and forwarders.

The progression moves learners from practice with machine controls into real forest terrain, where tree selection and changing ground add decisions that a simulator alone cannot reproduce. Participant Logan Gray, who already had excavator experience, described discovering how much terrain and tree differences matter. For instructors, the established program offers an example of sequencing practice and working with an employer to define the job. The college’s account does not establish current enrollment availability or a verified placement rate.

Operations & Costs

Woods to Wheels puts forestry fuel costs in focus

The Forest Business School’s Woods to Wheels: Every Gallon Counts brings together U.S. and Canadian research on timber-harvesting and log-trucking fuel use, with linked studies and practical calculators. Its workbench separates in-woods fuel and production from first-haul distance, payload, fuel economy and empty travel.

For an instructor-led exercise, students can change diesel price, calculate harvesting fuel cost per green short ton from the stated fuel rate, and compare it with the haul calculator’s cost per delivered green short ton. Keep U.S. gallons, green short tons and each activity boundary explicit. Historical examples and modeled assumptions need local fuel, production, payload and route records before they describe an actual operation; changing price alone does not estimate current-year fuel consumption.

Publications & Tools

U.S. — CLASSROOM AND FIELD / AVAILABLE TEACHING RESOURCE

A paired tree-measurement exercise makes discrepancies visible

Penn State’s Forest Measurements lesson puts students in pairs to compare diameter-tape readings with Biltmore-stick estimates. Activity 3 records tree species, diameter, merchantable logs and estimated board feet on a printable tally sheet, then has teams discuss differences in their results.

Written for grades 10–12, the available lesson can inform an introductory college field lab. A useful check before students use calculated volumes is to remeasure discrepancies between the two diameter readings. The exercise connects measurement technique with the timber quantities used by landowners, foresters and buyers. It is an existing lesson, not a newly published curriculum.
