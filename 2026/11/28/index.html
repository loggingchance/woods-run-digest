CANADA — NOVEMBER 27 / COMMUNITY ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT

Canada opens a diversification route for forestry-dependent communities

Canada’s new Forestry Regions Economic Diversification initiative commits C$100 million over three years to help forestry-dependent communities plan new economic activity and carry those plans into projects. Six regional development agencies will deliver it as part of the national Forest Sector Strategy announced November 27.

The practical route varies by region. Quebec lists applications as open, with eligible communities identified by regional county municipality. Economic-development nonprofits and qualifying small and medium-sized businesses can participate when projects contribute to community diversification. In British Columbia, PacifiCan asks organizations to discuss proposals directly; its C$22.1 million allocation has limited funding this fiscal year, with support increasing for projects beginning April 2027 or later.

The focus is broader local economic resilience. PacifiCan directs businesses primarily seeking tariff-related modernization or competitiveness support toward the separate Regional Tariff Response Initiative. Local governments, Indigenous organizations and development groups should start with their regional agency’s terms; the announcement does not establish awards or guarantee assistance to an individual mill.

Forestry & Wood Products Profile

Bill Hull’s path from barrel staves to hardwood lumber

Bill Hull’s father ran a backyard sawmill using trees blown down in the 1938 hurricane. In his April 13 Northern Woodlands interview, Hull follows that early connection to wood through logging, forestry school and a business of his own. He took over his father’s mill and cut white oak barrel staves for J.H. Hamlen & Son. After moving the operation to Pomfret, Connecticut, in 1970, he stocked white oak during fall and winter to saw during mud season. Then two employees left for other jobs one spring. Hull shifted the mill to graded hardwood lumber and needed a separate logging crew while he stayed at the sawmill. His account shows how available labor helped change both the product and the organization of the work—decisions made decades ago, recalled in this year’s interview.

SOUTH CAROLINA — BIDS OPEN MONDAY, NOVEMBER 30

Manchester offers a 152-acre pine thinning

South Carolina’s Forestry Commission opens bids for Manchester State Forest sale MSF-654-ADV-TS-27 at 10 a.m. Eastern on November 30. The Sumter County offering is an operator-select second thinning, with approximately 152 acres and an estimated 8,839 tons of pine. Buyers must make their own volume estimates.

The packet requires one blended price per ton for all pine products and lists a US$5,000 performance bond and US$25,000 advance. Bids may be mailed, hand-delivered or emailed. Review the map, crew-training requirements and submission instructions before bidding. The agency webpage calls November 30 a Tuesday; the bid packet and procurement notice correctly identify Monday.

WESTERN U.S. — NOVEMBER 25 FOREST RESEARCH

Tree counts and big-tree losses tell different elevation stories

A new Nature paper uses repeated measurements of 44,368 western U.S. forest-inventory plots to examine how warming, drought, fire, insects and disease redistribute trees across elevation. In its main analysis of plots without silvicultural treatment, shifts weighted by trunk basal area were more often downhill, while shifts based on stem counts varied more.

Larger-tree losses and smaller-tree recovery therefore give different views of change. Including treated plots made uphill and downhill basal-area shifts more evenly distributed. The observational study supports tracking tree size, regeneration and disturbance together; it does not show that every species is moving downhill or prescribe a harvest treatment.
